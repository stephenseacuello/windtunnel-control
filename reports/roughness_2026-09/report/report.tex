% --------------------------------------------------------------- abstract --
\section*{Abstract}

Fuzzy skin is a slicer setting that displaces the outer wall of a 3D print by a random distance up
to a set thickness~\cite{prusa}; it changes a printed blade's surface texture without changing its
CAD model. If texture changes a printed rotor's output, print settings must be controlled whenever
rotors are compared. Roughness degrades the lift of airfoil sections~\cite{wilcox} and lowers the
Reynolds number at which a circular cylinder's drag falls~\cite{achenbach}; neither result predicts
its effect on the thin curved-plate blades of the small vertical-axis rotor studied here. We tested
this rotor in the URI wind tunnel on 1 October 2026, fitted in turn with four blade sets: without
fuzzy skin (Plain) and with fuzzy-skin thicknesses of \SIlist{0.050;0.101;0.202}{mm} (FS~0.05,
FS~0.10, FS~0.20). Each rotor was swept twice through \nSP{} fan speeds (\vLo--\SI{\vHi}{m/s} by
the tunnel calibration) with an automated constant-current load; light-load rotor speed and surface
roughness were also measured. Averaged over wind speed, FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 produced $\cPB\%$
$[\cPBlo,\ \cPBhi]$ and $\cPC\%$ $[\cPClo,\ \cPChi]$ more peak electrical power than Plain (Tukey
simultaneous 95\% intervals); the FS~0.05 difference, $\cPA\%$ $[\cPAlo,\ \cPAhi]$, was not
resolved, its interval including zero. The gains were largest at \bandLo--\SI{\bandHi}{m/s}.
Averaged over wind speed, they came with a higher extrapolated open-circuit voltage and no resolved
change in apparent source resistance; where they were largest, a lower source resistance also
contributed. At light load, averaged over \vSpdLo--\SI{\vHi}{m/s}, FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 turned
$\spdPB\%$ and $\spdPC\%$ faster than Plain, with no resolved difference in voltage per rpm; we
infer that the gains arise in the rotor, not in the generator. Measured roughness (Ra, Pa) did not
rank the sets by power. Each setting was one print, mounted once and tested in order of increasing
thickness, and Plain also has a coarser layer period (\SI{\pitchP}{mm} against \SI{\pitchA}{mm}),
so attributing the differences to fuzzy skin requires replicate prints, remounting and an
interleaved run order. Until then, rotors compared on this rig, including in the planned fault
tests, should be printed from one recorded set of print settings.

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:scope}

Printed blades carry surface texture even without fuzzy skin: the Plain set tested here has
\SI{\pitchP}{mm} layer lines (\secref{sec:roughness}). If texture changes rotor output, print
settings must be controlled in any experiment that compares rotors, including the fault tests
planned for this rig (\secref{sec:faults}), and texture becomes a design variable. Fuzzy skin makes
the texture adjustable without changing the CAD geometry. In PrusaSlicer it resamples each
outer-wall path with a random step size and shifts each new point inside or outside the wall by a
random distance up to the fuzzy-skin thickness~\cite{prusa}; the Bambu Studio project used for
these blades stores the same two parameters, thickness and point distance (\secref{sec:sets}).

On airfoil blade sections at Reynolds numbers of 1.6--4.0$\times10^6$, increasing roughness height
and density decreases the maximum lift, lift-curve slope and lift-to-drag ratio~\cite{wilcox}. On a
circular cylinder, roughness lowers the critical Reynolds number, at which the drag falls, leaves
the subcritical drag unchanged and raises the transcritical drag~\cite{achenbach}. The blades tested
here are thin curved plates with square edges at chord Reynolds numbers of $\reLo\times10^4$ to
$\reHi\times10^5$ (\secref{sec:rotor}). Neither result predicts the sign or size of a texture effect
on such blades, so it was measured.

One rotor was fitted in turn with the four blade sets and tested at \nSP{} wind speeds, twice per
set, to answer four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Does peak electrical power differ between the sets? Tukey simultaneous intervals on run
        means~\cite{montgomery} (\secref{sec:comparison}; conclusion~1).
  \item Do the differences depend on wind speed? Rotor $\times$ set point interaction
        (\secref{sec:windspeed}; conclusion~2).
  \item Does a difference arise in the rotor or in the generator? A Thévenin fit of each load
        ladder, light-load rotor speed and voltage per rpm (Sections~\ref{sec:thevenin}
        and~\ref{sec:speed}; conclusion~3).
  \item Does measured surface roughness order the sets by power? Profilometer Pa and Ra
        (\secref{sec:roughness}; conclusion~4).
\end{enumerate}
The report covers the 1 October 2026 session only; earlier laboratory (June--July) and rig
(August--September) tests are outside its scope. A fifth blade set run that day is excluded because
its run log records its texture as unknown. The report also responds to two earlier requests: blade
identity (\tabref{tab:specimens}) and an outline for a fault-testing plan (\secref{sec:faults}).
Data and code accompany it (\appref{app:package}).

\section{Test articles}\label{sec:specimens}

\subsection{Rotor}\label{sec:rotor}
The rotor has three straight, untwisted blades attached at $R=\SI{\geoR}{m}$ from the axis, with
span $H=\SI{\geoH}{m}$. The blade section is a thin curved plate that turns through
\SI{\geoArc}{\degree} (\SI{\geoChord}{mm} chord, \SI{\geoDepth}{mm} depth, \SI{\geoWall}{mm} wall,
square edges); it is not an airfoil. These are design values; the printed blades were not measured.
The outer swept radius is larger than $R$ and was not documented, so the nominal swept area
$A=2RH=\SI{\areaSw}{m^2}$ is used only to normalise power. Chord Reynolds numbers use the wind speed
and assume air at \SI{20}{\celsius}; air temperature was not recorded.

Plain also differs in layer period. The earlier labels identify the files: Plain's runs are

\subsection{Surface measurement}\label{sec:surface}
Each set was scanned on 1 October before the runs with a Keyence VR-6000
optical profilometer (VR-6100 head, $\times$160): one field of about \fieldX\,$\times$\,\fieldY\,mm
on one blade per set, two on FS~0.20, at \SI{\pixel}{\micro m} per pixel (\figref{fig:surface}).
Field locations on the blades were not recorded. Each spanwise profile (across the layer lines) is
levelled. Pa is the arithmetic mean deviation of the levelled profile; Ra is that of the roughness
profile, the levelled profile minus its Gaussian mean line (ISO~16610-21~\cite{iso16610},
$\lambda_c=\SI{0.25}{mm}$), over a \evalLo--\SI{\evalHi}{mm} evaluation length, shorter than the
default of five cut-off lengths (\SI{1.25}{mm})~\cite{iso4288}; \appref{app:surface} gives the
details. Each value is the mean over a field's pixel columns; adjacent columns are correlated, so the
field is the sampling unit.

% ============================================================== 3 FACILITY ==
\section{Facility and instrumentation}\label{sec:facility}

\subsection{Wind speed}\label{sec:wind}
The tunnel fan is driven by an ABB ACS550 variable-frequency drive, commanded in rpm by a Portenta
Machine Control over Modbus (\figref{fig:rig}). Wind speed $v$ is calculated from the commanded fan
speed $N$ with the tunnel's calibration (Test~1, 13 February 2026), a least-squares line through
\calN{} points (\appref{app:cal}):
\[ v = \calA\,N - \calB \qquad (\si{m/s};\ N \text{ in rpm}). \]
Of these points, \calNmeas{} were measured, from 0 to \calMeasRpm~rpm (up to \SI{\calMeasV}{m/s});
the calibration file marks the other \calNtrend{} (\calTrendLo--\calTrendHi~rpm) as possibly read
from the trend line of the original record, and the Test~1 instrument is not documented. The wind
speeds assigned to the \nSPabove{} set points above \calMeasRpm~rpm (\vAboveLo--\SI{\vHi}{m/s})
therefore rest on the assumption that air speed stays linear in fan speed. At \spLo--\calMeasRpm~rpm the line is \calLowLo--\calLowHi\% above
the measured speeds; a line through the measured points alone gives \SI{\calMeasTop}{m/s} at
1800~rpm, \calDiffTop\% below the adopted value. The calibration affects absolute wind speed and
$C_{P,\mathrm{el}}$, not the comparison between rotors, which are compared at identical fan speed.

The median fan-motor current at each set point agreed across the \nRunsWord{} runs within
\SI{\fanRange}{A}, the drive's reading resolution (\SI{\fanTop}{A} at 1800~rpm), so the fan
operating point did not change measurably. Air temperature and pressure were not recorded. No
blockage correction is applied; blockage is assumed to act alike on rotors of one geometry.

\subsection{Electrical measurement}
The rotor shaft drives a three-phase generator whose output passes through a three-phase bridge
rectifier to a Chroma 63004-150-60 DC electronic load in constant-current mode. The generator's
make, model and winding resistance were not recorded. A Python host sets the load current and
records the terminal voltage $V$ and current $I$ that the load reports at the end of each dwell;
electrical power is $P=VI$. All rotors were measured with the same instrument at the same settings,
so an instrument gain error cancels in the ratios between rotors.

\subsection{Rotor speed}\label{sec:rpm}
The rig's rotor-speed input counted no pulses on 1 October. T.~Kang recorded rotor speed on a
separate data acquisition system from a tachometer signal sampled at $f_s=\SI{\tachoFs}{Hz}$; the
sensor type and target were not recorded. His script (\texttt{RPM.m}, in the data package) treats
each pulse as one revolution, splits each run into \nSP{} segments at rises in the \SI{5}{s} mean
speed, assigns them to the set points in order, and reports for each the most frequent
per-revolution speed $n_0$, rounded to 1~rpm. The summaries carry no time stamps. Because a pulse
interval is a whole number of samples, $n_0$ is resolved to the larger of 1~rpm and
$n_0^2/(60f_s)$, that is \resMin--\resHi\% of $n_0$.

We take $n_0$ as the light-load speed, the rotor speed with the generator unloaded: the load is held
at \SI{0}{A} while the fan settles (\secref{sec:procedure}), and the rotor then turns at its highest
and steadiest speed of the set point. From \spdSpLo~rpm the rig's record supports this and the
matching of set points. The terminal voltage at the first load step (\iFirstLo--\SI{\iFirstHi}{mA}),
$V_1$, follows the least-squares line $V_1=\SI{\keSlope}{mV/rpm}\times n_0-\SI{\keIcept}{V}$ over
the \nSpdPoints{} rotor, run and set-point values (\figref{fig:speed}c), and $V_1/n_0$ averages
\SI{\keMean}{mV/rpm} with a coefficient of variation of \keCV\%. At the first set point, \spLo~rpm,
the fan settles with the load at \SI{\loadArmmA}{mA} instead, and the speed that $V_1$ implies on
this line exceeds $n_0$ by \gapLo--\gapHi\%; \spLo~rpm is therefore not used for speed. Rotor speed
at each load dwell was not recorded.

% ============================================================= 4 PROCEDURE ==
\section{Test procedure}\label{sec:procedure}

\subsection{Sweep protocol}
Each run (sweep) visits \nSP{} fan set points from \spLo{} to \spHi~rpm in 100~rpm steps. At each
set point the host waits until the fan speed is within the larger of \rpmTolAbs~rpm and
\rpmTolPct\% of the command, then until the generator's terminal voltage changes by no more than
\settleTolPct\% (or \SI{0.02}{V}, whichever is larger) over each of \settleConfirm{} successive
\SI{\settlePoll}{s} intervals; the voltage wait lasts at least \SI{\settleMin}{s}, and each wait at
most \SI{\settleMax}{s}. While the fan settles the load is at \SI{0}{A}, except at the first set
point, where it stays at the \SI{\loadArmmA}{mA} set before the fan starts. The load then raises its
current demand from half a step, in steps of \SI{\stepmA}{mA} at 1800~rpm scaled with $v^2$ at lower
speeds (minimum \SI{\stepMinmA}{mA}), dwelling \SI{\dwellS}{s} per step, and stops after
\stopConfirm{} consecutive dwells at or below \stopPct\% of the running maximum. The resulting
ladders of \dwellLo--\dwellHi{} dwells trace the power--current curve through its maximum
(\figref{fig:ladders}). A run takes about \runMin{} minutes; all runs used the same protocol
(identifier \texttt{94bed28333f7}).

\subsection{Data quality}\label{sec:quality}
Of the \nSweeps{} ladders, \nClean{} stopped on the power roll-off. The other \nCut{} stopped after
the peak on the host's voltage floor: the terminal voltage fell below \SI{\vOff}{V} on
\stopConfirm{} consecutive dwells while the current still followed the demand. In these two ladders,
\cutList{}, the last dwell above the floor had already fallen below \stopPct\% of the maximum
(percentages in parentheses), so the peak lies inside every ladder. Peak powers recomputed from the dwell records equal the values logged
by the rig. One Plain blade broke at the end of run~2; at the last set point, 1800~rpm, $\pmax$
agrees with run~1 within \breakDiff\%. One FS~0.10 blade carried a small sensor magnet in an earlier
test; whether it was fitted on 1 October was not recorded.

% ============================================================ 5 REDUCTION ==
\section{Data reduction and statistical analysis}\label{sec:stats}

\textbf{Peak power.} $\pmax$ is the largest dwell power in a ladder. A least-squares parabola through
the maximum and up to two dwells on either side (the rig software's rule) gives a second estimate;
in \nFallback{} ladders the fit is not concave or its vertex lies outside those dwells, and the
maximum is used. The two estimators resolve the same pairs (\tabref{tab:pairs}). Because dwells
last \SI{\dwellS}{s} and speed was not recorded per dwell, rotor equilibrium within a dwell is not
shown. At \spLo~rpm, which settles with the load at
\SI{\loadArmmA}{mA}, the terminal voltage rose between the first two dwells in \nRise{} of
\nRunsWord{} runs, so the rotor was still accelerating when the ladder began. $\pmax$ is the peak of this ramp protocol.

\textbf{Rotor comparison.} Differences are analysed as ratios, on $\ln\pmax$. For each run, $y$ is the
mean over the \nSP{} set points of $\ln(\pmax/P_\text{ref})$, with $P_\text{ref}$ the geometric mean
of the two Plain runs at that set point, so each wind speed carries equal weight. The pooled
run-to-run standard deviation of $y$ is $\sigma=\sigRun\%$ (\dofRun{} degrees of freedom). The rotor
effect is tested by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) on the \nRunsWord{} run means, and all six
pairs are compared with Tukey's honestly significant difference procedure (simultaneous 95\%
intervals; studentized range $q_{0.05;4,\dofRun}=\qTukey$)~\cite{montgomery}. A difference is
resolved when its interval excludes zero. With two runs per rotor the $p$-values rest on a normal
model and are reported down to 0.001; an exact permutation test of the rotor effect gives
$p=\permP$, the smallest attainable. Because each rotor's two runs were consecutive, neither test
separates a rotor effect from drift (\secref{sec:limits}).

\textbf{Mounting and drift.} The two runs of a rotor share its print and mounting, so the intervals
measure repeatability on one mounting. Mounting-to-mounting variation $\tau$, the standard
deviation of a rotor's mean power between mountings, was not measured. For each resolved difference
the largest $\tau$ at which it would stay resolved by the Tukey criterion is calculated
(\appref{app:stats}). Because the rotors were run in order of increasing texture, the run means are
also refitted with a linear time term (\secref{sec:limits}).

\textbf{Dependence on wind speed.} A two-way ANOVA with runs nested in rotors tests the rotor
$\times$ set point interaction against the residual (run $\times$ set point within rotor), that is,
whether the ratio between rotors changes with wind speed by more than the scatter of repeated runs
(\appref{app:stats}). The residual scatter differs between set points, so the $F$ test is
approximate; it is repeated without the two set points with the largest scatter.

\textbf{Open-circuit voltage and source resistance.} At each set point a straight line
$V=V_\mathrm{oc}-IR_\mathrm{int}$ (a Thévenin equivalent) is fitted to the dwells above the voltage
floor ($r^2\ge\thevRtwo$). The fit separates a change in generated voltage from a change in the
voltage drop under load. $V_\mathrm{oc}$, the zero-current intercept, is an extrapolated
open-circuit voltage, not a measured one: from \vocBelowFrom~rpm its mean over all runs lies
\vocBelowMeanLo--\vocBelowMeanHi\% below $V_1$ (single runs up to \vocBelowMax\%), so the measured
voltage falls faster over the first dwells than the fitted line. $R_\mathrm{int}$ is an apparent
source resistance: it includes the winding, the rectifier with its commutation drop, the wiring, and
the slowing of the rotor as the load rises. For Plain, $R_\mathrm{int}$ falls from
\rintPlainLo\,$\Omega$ at \SI{\vLo}{m/s} to \rintPlainHi\,$\Omega$ at \SI{\vHi}{m/s}. A winding or
wiring resistance does not fall with speed, and the commutation drop of a diode bridge rises with
frequency~\cite[ch.~5]{mohan}. We therefore attribute the fall to the rotor slowing under load and
to the diodes, whose incremental resistance falls as the ladder current rises; the diode type was
not recorded, so the two are not separated. As a check of the linear model,
$V_\mathrm{oc}^2/4R_\mathrm{int}$, the maximum power of the fitted source, is \matchLo--\matchHi{}
times $\pmax$ over all ladders. Both parameters are compared between rotors as $\pmax$ is.

\textbf{Rotor speed.} $\ln n_0$ at fan set points \spdSpLo--\spHi~rpm (\vSpdLo--\SI{\vHi}{m/s}) is
compared between rotors as $\ln\pmax$ is
($\sigma=\sigSpd\%$). Where both runs of a rotor give the same quantised value (\nSameQ{} of
\nPairsQ{} cases), the quantisation error is shared and not in $\sigma$, so the $n_0$ intervals may
be too narrow; $V_1$, which is continuous, resolves the same \nSpdResolved{} pairs. $\ln V_1$ and the
voltage per rpm, $\ln(V_1/n_0)$ and $\ln(V_\mathrm{oc}/n_0)$, are compared in the same way. The
light-load tip-speed ratio is
$\lambda_0=2\pi n_0R/(60\,v)$ ($n_0$ in rpm, $R$ in m, $v$ in m/s). It exceeds the tip-speed ratio
at peak power, where the load slows the rotor, and it uses the attachment radius $R$, so the ratio
at the larger outer radius is higher.

\textbf{Power coefficient.} $C_{P,\mathrm{el}}=\pmax/(\tfrac12\rho A v^3)$, with standard air
($\rho=\SI{\rhoStd}{kg/m^3}$), normalises power for wind speed. It includes generator and rectifier
losses and is not the aerodynamic power coefficient.

\subsection{Power curves}

\subsection{Rotor comparison}\label{sec:comparison}

resolved; FS~0.05 vs Plain is not. The smallest resolved difference, FS~0.20 over FS~0.10 ($\cBC\%$

($p\pfBC$). These intervals measure repeatability on one mounting. The differences of FS~0.10 and
FS~0.20 from Plain would stay resolved for a mounting variation $\tau$ up to \tauPB\% and \tauPC\%,

\subsection{Dependence on wind speed}\label{sec:windspeed}
The rotor differences change with wind speed (\figref{fig:gain}). The rotor $\times$ set point
interaction exceeds the scatter of repeated runs ($F_{\dfIntA,\dfIntB}=\Fint$,~$p\pInt$), also among
the three textured rotors alone ($F_{\dfIntTexA,\dfIntTexB}=\FintTex$,~$p\pIntTex$) and without the
two set points with the largest scatter, \trimDropped~rpm
($F_{\dfIntTrimA,\dfIntTrimB}=\FintTrim$,~$p\pIntTrim$). The gains are largest at

% ============================================================ 7 DISCUSSION ==
\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{Where the power difference arises.} We infer that the power difference arises in the rotor,
not in the generator. Averaged over wind speed, the gain comes with a higher $V_\mathrm{oc}$ and no
resolved change in $R_\mathrm{int}$ (\secref{sec:thevenin}). The light-load speed rises by the same
fraction as $V_\mathrm{oc}$, and neither voltage per rpm shows a resolved difference between rotors
(\secref{sec:speed}). The generator therefore converts speed to voltage alike for every rotor, to
within \keBound\%, and the textured rotors turn faster. With the same $V$--$I$ slope and a higher
$V_\mathrm{oc}$, they also turn faster at equal load current, that is, at about equal generator
torque. The data do not separate a higher aerodynamic torque from a lower resisting torque. All
rotors used the same generator and shaft bearings, so a lower resisting torque would have to come
from the blade sets (mass, balance or mounting, none measured; the only documented difference is the
FS~0.10 sensor magnet of \secref{sec:quality}) or from a change in the shared parts between
rotors, which the run order does not exclude (\secref{sec:limits}). The $R_\mathrm{int}$ pattern
that FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 share (\secref{sec:thevenin}) may come from the Plain reference rather than
from the texture.

\textbf{Wind-speed dependence.} The gains of FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 peak at chord Reynolds numbers of
$\reBandLo$--$\reBandHi\times10^4$, their power rises most steeply at a lower wind speed than
Plain's (\secref{sec:windspeed}), and their light-load speed gain is larger from
\SI{\spdSplitHi}{m/s} (\secref{sec:speed}). A flow transition that depends on Reynolds number and
that texture moves to a lower wind speed could produce this pattern. On a circular cylinder,
roughness lowers the critical Reynolds number, leaves the subcritical drag unchanged and raises the
transcritical drag~\cite{achenbach}, so its effect depends on the Reynolds-number range. The flow on
these blades was not examined, so this remains a hypothesis.

\textbf{Measured roughness.} Ra and Pa do not rank the rotors by power (\secref{sec:roughness}).
Four explanations remain open: the realised texture of FS~0.05 and FS~0.10 may not follow the
setting; one field per set may not resolve it; Pa and Ra may not capture the surface property that
matters for this flow; or the power differences may not come from texture. With one print per level,
the agreement between power and nominal thickness is an association (\secref{sec:limits}).

\textbf{Operating regime and losses.} The light-load tip-speed ratio does not exceed \lamMaxAll{}
(\secref{sec:speed}). A blade driven by drag alone stops producing driving force when it moves as
fast as the wind, so this range is consistent with drag-driven operation. $\lambda_0$ also rises by
\lamRiseLo--\lamRiseHi\% between \vSpdLo{} and \SI{\vHi}{m/s}. If the aerodynamic torque
coefficient depended on $\lambda$ alone, a rotor without resisting torque would run unloaded at a
fixed $\lambda$. The rise is therefore consistent with a resisting torque (bearings and generator
no-load losses) that is a larger share of the aerodynamic torque at low wind speed; a dependence of
the aerodynamic torque on Reynolds number could also contribute. The largest $C_{P,\mathrm{el}}$,
\cpHi\%, is \akwaLo--\akwaHi{} times below the maximum averaged power coefficients of about
0.05--0.30 reported for Savonius rotors~\cite{akwa}. The comparison is indicative only: this rotor
differs from a Savonius rotor in geometry, and $C_{P,\mathrm{el}}$ includes generator and rectifier
losses. If those losses were equal for all rotors at a given wind speed, a fractional change in
aerodynamic power would appear magnified in $\pmax$, and the measured gains would overstate the
aerodynamic change. Speed-dependent losses would, however, be larger for the faster textured rotors,
which acts the other way; the data do not separate the two.

% ================================================================ 8 LIMITS ==
\section{Uncertainty and limitations}\label{sec:limits}

The Tukey intervals contain only run-to-run repeatability on one mounting. Two further sources are
confounded with texture:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Print and mounting.} Each texture level is one print mounted once, so print-to-print
        and mounting variation are confounded with texture; \secref{sec:comparison} gives the
        mounting variation each difference could absorb. Plain also differs in layer period
        (\SI{\pitchP}{mm} against \SI{\pitchA}{mm}) and has no slicer record. The three fuzzy-skin
        sets share \SI{\slLayer}{mm} layers, so the comparisons among them, FS~0.10 and FS~0.20
        over FS~0.05 ($\cAB\%$ $[\cABlo,\ \cABhi]$ and $\cAC\%$ $[\cAClo,\ \cAChi]$), are not
        affected by layer height.
  \item \textbf{Run order.} The rotors were run in order of increasing texture, so slow drift is
        confounded with texture. A fit of the \nRunsWord{} run means with a rotor term and a linear
        time term gives a drift of $\driftRate\%$ per hour (95\% interval $\driftLo\%$ to
        $\driftHi\%$). With that term FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 remain resolved from Plain ($\cdB\%$
        $[\cdBlo,\ \cdBhi]$ and $\cdC\%$ $[\cdClo,\ \cdChi]$; $t$ intervals, \driftDof{} degrees of
        freedom), while FS~0.05 is $\cdA\%$ $[\cdAlo,\ \cdAhi]$. Under a linear-drift model the
        FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 differences therefore persist, while the FS~0.05 difference could be
        drift. The slope rests almost entirely (\driftShare\% of the leverage) on the two Plain runs,
        the only pair an hour apart, and a change between rotors, rather than a steady drift, cannot
        be separated from a rotor effect.
\end{itemize}

\FloatBarrier
\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 produced $\cPB\%$ $[\cPBlo,\ \cPBhi]$ and $\cPC\%$ $[\cPClo,\ \cPChi]$ more
        peak electrical power than Plain, averaged over \vLo--\vHi\,\si{m/s}. The FS~0.05
        difference, $\cPA\%$ $[\cPAlo,\ \cPAhi]$, was not resolved and could result from session
        drift.
  \item The gains depended on wind speed and were largest at \bandLo--\SI{\bandHi}{m/s}, where
        FS~0.20 reached $\gMaxC\%$. The power of FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 rose most steeply at
        \SI{\stepVB}{m/s} and \SI{\stepVC}{m/s}, below Plain's \stepVP\,\si{m/s}.
  \item Averaged over wind speed, the gains came with a higher extrapolated open-circuit voltage and
        no resolved change in apparent source resistance; where they were largest, a lower source
        resistance also contributed. At light load FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 turned $\spdPB\%$
        $[\spdPBlo,\ \spdPBhi]$ and $\spdPC\%$ $[\spdPClo,\ \spdPChi]$ faster than Plain, with no
        resolved difference in voltage per rpm. We infer that the difference arises in the rotor,
        not in the generator.
  \item Ra and Pa from one field per set did not rank the rotors by power and do not confirm that
        the realised texture of FS~0.05 and FS~0.10 differs.
  \item The intervals reflect repeatability on one print and one mounting per texture, and Plain
        also differs in layer period. The FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 differences from Plain would remain
        resolved for mounting variation up to \tauPB--\tauPC\% and under a linear-drift fit;
        attributing them to fuzzy skin requires replicate prints, remounting and an interleaved run
        order. The fuzzy-skin sets share \SI{\slLayer}{mm} layers, so FS~0.10 and FS~0.20 over
        FS~0.05, $\cAB\%$ $[\cABlo,\ \cABhi]$ and $\cAC\%$ $[\cAClo,\ \cAChi]$, are not affected by
        layer height.
\end{enumerate}

% ================================================================= 10 NEXT ==
\section{Recommendations}\label{sec:next}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Replicate and randomise.} Print at least two sets per thickness, Plain included, from
        one saved project (\SI{0.1}{mm} layers, \SI{0.2}{mm} nozzle, one printer and filament lot;
        Print Lab or in house), in random order. Keep the project file and print record, and weigh
        and label every blade, including the present sets, so that mass and balance, the alternative
        to texture in \secref{sec:discussion}, are known. Mount each set twice and run in an
        interleaved order, re-running Plain after every third rotor. This measures print and
        mounting variation and breaks the run-order confound.
  \item \textbf{Log rotor speed at every dwell.} Connect a once-per-revolution sensor to the rig's
        pulse input, or put T.~Kang's acquisition on the rig clock, and compute speed as revolutions
        over elapsed time rather than the mode of single-revolution speeds. T.~Kang's per-sample
        records of the present runs, not yet retrieved, could give speed at every dwell if they can
        be aligned to the rig's time stamps.
        Per-dwell speed gives $C_{P,\mathrm{el}}(\lambda)$ along each ladder and separates the
        electrical source impedance (winding, rectifier, commutation) from the rotor slowing. Also
        log the settled \SI{0}{A} voltage, which the rig measures but does not save, measure the
        generator's winding resistance and identify the rectifier diodes. Separating aerodynamic
        from resisting torque also requires a spin-down or motored friction test of each mounted
        rotor.
  \item \textbf{Extend the wind calibration.} Measure air speed directly over the test range, record
        air temperature and pressure on every run, and document the test-section dimensions for a
        blockage estimate.
  \item \textbf{Characterise the surfaces at fixed stations}: several fields per set on two blades
        and both faces, profiles along and across the layer lines, and evaluation lengths of at
        least five cut-off lengths.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Toward the fault-testing plan}\label{sec:faults}
Prof.\ Jeong asked for a fault-testing plan agreed before the next round. The rig provides an
automated sweep of about \runMin{} minutes with a run-to-run repeatability of \sigRun\% in the run
mean and \sigE\% at a single set point, measured on repeat runs of one mounting. Blade sets printed
with different settings differed by up to $\cPC\%$ in mean peak power (\secref{sec:comparison}),
so fault blades and their reference set should be printed from one saved project, and the reference
re-run between fault cases. Whether $V_\mathrm{oc}$ and $R_\mathrm{int}$ separate rotor-side from
electrical faults is untested. $R_\mathrm{int}$ also reflects the rotor slowing under load
(\secref{sec:stats}), so a rotor-side fault may change it as an electrical fault would; the first
fault cases should test this with rotor speed logged at every dwell. Candidate faults are a tip mass on one blade (imbalance), a chipped, shortened or
missing blade, added bearing drag, and an open or high-resistance generator phase. A short meeting
to agree the fault list, severity levels and acceptance criteria should precede any fault prints.

\section{Statistical details}\label{app:stats}
\textbf{Model.} With $y_{rjs}=\ln\pmax$ for rotor $r$ (4), run $j$ (2) and set point $s$ (14),
\[ y_{rjs} = \mu + \alpha_r + \beta_s + (\alpha\beta)_{rs} + u_{rj} + e_{rjs}, \]
where $u_{rj}$ is a run offset within the rotor and $e_{rjs}$ the residual~\cite{montgomery}. The
rotor effect is tested against run within rotor, which is the one-way ANOVA on run means. The
interaction $(\alpha\beta)_{rs}$ and the run term are tested against the residual
(\tabref{tab:anova}). The run offsets $u_{rj}$ cancel from both the interaction and the residual
mean squares, so the interaction test needs no assumption about them; it does assume equal residual
variance, so it is approximate here.

\textbf{Comparisons.} With run means $\bar y_{rj}$, rotor means $\bar y_r$ and pooled
$\sigma^2=\sum_r\sum_j(\bar y_{rj}-\bar y_r)^2/4$, Tukey's interval for rotors $a$ and $b$ is
$\exp\!\big(\bar y_b-\bar y_a \pm q_{0.05;4,4}\,\sigma/\sqrt2\big)-1$, a half-width of \hwTukeyLog{}
in $\ln\pmax$ (about \hwTukey\%). The same procedure is applied to $\ln V_\mathrm{oc}$ and
$\ln R_\mathrm{int}$, and to $\ln n_0$, $\ln V_1$, $\ln(V_1/n_0)$ and $\ln(V_\mathrm{oc}/n_0)$ at
fan set points \spdSpLo--\spHi~rpm. The
permutation test enumerates all \permN{} ways of splitting the \nRunsWord{} run means into four
pairs; for $\ln n_0$ it also gives $p=\permP$.

\textbf{Mounting variation.} If each rotor's mean carried an independent mounting offset with
standard deviation $\tau$, the difference of two rotor means would have variance $\sigma^2+2\tau^2$.
It stays resolved by the Tukey criterion while
$|\bar y_b-\bar y_a|>(q_{0.05;4,4}/\sqrt2)\sqrt{\sigma^2+2\tau^2}$, which gives the values of $\tau$
in \secref{sec:stats}.

\textbf{Drift.} The run means are fitted as $\bar y_{rj}=a_r+\beta t_{rj}$, with $t_{rj}$ the start
time of the run in hours, by least squares (\driftDof{} residual degrees of freedom).

\textbf{Residual scatter.} The per-point residual standard deviation is \sigE\% overall; it is
largest at \sigEmaxRpm~rpm (\sigEmax\%) and smallest at \sigEminRpm~rpm (\sigEmin\%).

\section{Surface analysis}\label{app:surface}
The Keyence export is a height map in millimetres, quantised to \SI{1}{\micro m}; each image column
is one spanwise profile. A column is dropped if more than 10\% of its points are missing, or more
than 2\% between its first and last valid points: \rejLo\% of columns or fewer for Plain, FS~0.05 and
FS~0.10, and \rejC\% for FS~0.20, where missing points concentrate on steep slopes, so its values
may be understated. Remaining interior gaps are filled linearly, a least-squares line is removed,
and the profiles of a field are cut to the length of the shortest. Pa is the mean absolute
deviation of the levelled profile. For Ra, the levelled profile minus its Gaussian mean line
(ISO~16610-21~\cite{iso16610}; $\alpha=\sqrt{\ln2/\pi}$, $\lambda_c=\SI{0.25}{mm}$) is evaluated
with $\lambda_c/2$ discarded at each end, where the filter has end effects. The layer period is the peak of the mean power spectrum between 60 and
\SI{320}{\micro m}. For periodic profiles ISO~4288~\cite{iso4288} selects $\lambda_c=\SI{0.25}{mm}$
for spacings of 0.04--\SI{0.13}{mm}, which fits FS~0.05 and FS~0.10; the Plain set's \SI{0.2}{mm}
period would call for \SI{0.8}{mm}, and the FS~0.20 profile, which shows no layer period (Ra above
\SI{10}{\micro m}) for \SI{8}{mm}, both longer than the \fieldY\,mm field. The filter transmits
50\% at $\lambda_c$, so Ra keeps about \transP\% of the amplitude of the Plain set's
\SI{\pitchP}{mm} layer lines and \transA\% of the \SI{\pitchA}{mm} ones; Plain's Ra is therefore
understated relative to FS~0.05 and FS~0.10. Pa is unfiltered.
