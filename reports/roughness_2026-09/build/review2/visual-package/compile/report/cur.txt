Summary

Bottom line. On one mounting each, rotors printed with 0.10 and 0.20 mm of fuzzy-skin
texture produced about 8% and 14% more peak electrical power than the 0.05 mm rotor across
10.2–38.0 m/s. The extra power comes with the rotor turning faster in the same wind, not from
the electrical side. It is not yet shown that texture, rather than mounting, print differences or a
change between the first run and the others, is the cause. The remounts planned for 1 October are
the first check.

1 Objective and scope
The question is whether the surface texture of 3D-printed blades changes the power a small vertical-
axis wind turbine (VAWT) can deliver, and by how much. This report covers every texture test
run on the automated rig to date. It places the Jeong-lab tests of June and July in context, states
what the data do and do not support, and lists the measurements that would close the remaining
gaps. The accompanying data package contains every raw file used, unmodified, together with a
data dictionary, checksums and the analysis code (Appendix D). Every number in this report is
generated from those files.


2 Test articles

2.1 Rotor

The rotor is a three-bladed VAWT with straight blades on arms (Table 1). Its blade section is
not an airfoil. Each blade is a thin cambered plate: a constant 1.79 mm wall, about 24.5 mm deep
overall on a 48 mm chord, 183° of turning and square-cut edges, prismatic along the span with no
twist. In cross-section each blade is closer to a half-cylinder bucket than to a wing. Because a
VAWT sweeps a cylinder, the swept area is A = 2RH.

2.2 The texture specimens, and what is known about each

All textured rotors are printed copies of geometry v1, the first printed replica of the original rotor,
which the professors chose over a remodelled v2 on 4 August. They differ in the slicer’s fuzzy-skin
setting, which randomly displaces the outer wall. The controlled variable is the fuzzy-skin thickness,
and it doubles from one rotor to the next (Table 2).
Four facts about the specimens limit how the results can be read.

  This report uses fuzzy-skin thickness as the texture variable.
• The print details typed into the run notes are not reliable. The notes say “0.2mm layer”
  and, for Ra 80, “0.2mm nozzle”. The garage printer had only 0.4 mm nozzles throughout the

  are archived but have not yet been read.
• The prints came from more than one source. Nothing records which print file produced
  which rotor, and on 13 August two print sets were noted to “feel very different”. If the rotors
  differ in mass, wall thickness or edge finish as well as in texture, those differences are confounded
  with texture here.
• The Ra 40 rotor carried a small magnet on one blade for the reed switch; the others, as far
  as the records show, did not.

3.1 Rig

Fig. 2 shows the chain used for the rotor sweeps. A Python host commands the tunnel fan through
a Portenta Machine Control and an ABB ACS550 drive. The generator on the rotor shaft feeds a
three-phase bridge rectifier. A Chroma 63004 DC electronic load, controlled by the same host, sets
the load current and measures terminal voltage and current. Wind speed comes from the tunnel
calibration v = 0.02132 Nfan − 0.424 (m/s, fan speed Nfan in rpm, R2 = 0.9996), applied to the
commanded fan speed (Section 4).

3.2 Sweep protocol

All rig runs used one automated protocol. The software identifies it by the fingerprint 94bed28333f7,
which it computes from every setting that affects what a measurement means. For each of 14 fan
set points from 500 to 1800 rpm in steps of 100:
1. the fan is commanded, and the host waits for the drive to reach speed and for the terminal
   voltage to settle;
2. the load steps its constant-current demand upward, dwelling 1.0 s per step and recording voltage
   and current at the end of each dwell. The step is 20 mA at 1800 rpm and scales with v 2 at lower
   wind, so every wind speed gets a comparable number of steps;
3. the ramp stops at the first step where power falls below 80% of the running maximum (the last
   step in these runs was at 54–79% of the peak), the load is released, and the fan steps up.
A run takes about eight minutes.

3.3 Peak power: two estimators

Two estimates of Pmax come from each load ladder.
• The raw arg max is the largest single dwell.
• The parabolic fit is the vertex of a least-squares parabola through the arg max and the two dwells
  either side.
The top of P (I) is flat, so the arg max is biased slightly high. The Ra 20 run predates the logged
fit, so we recomputed both estimators for every run from its raw dwells, using the rig’s own rule
(Appendix B). The recomputed arg max values equal the logged ones exactly. The recomputed fits
match the logged Ra 40 and Ra 80 fits to within 0.14%, which is the rounding of the stored values.
The fit fell back to the arg max at 2 set points (Ra 20 at 700 rpm and Ra 40 at 600 rpm). The
headline numbers use the raw arg max; the two bases agree (Table 4).

3.4 Statistics

Rotors are compared at matched fan set points. For each pair the change is the geometric mean of
the 14 paired ratios. Two 95% intervals are given.
• The first uses the t distribution on the log-ratios and assumes the 14 set points are independent.
• They are not. Neighbouring set points share local deviations: the lag-1 autocorrelation of the
  log-ratios is 0.49 for Ra 80 against Ra 20. The second interval therefore uses an AR(1) effective
  sample size, and it is the one quoted in the Summary.
Both intervals describe scatter between wind speeds within one mounting of each rotor. Neither
includes mount-to-mount or print-to-print variation. Counts such as “higher at 14 of 14” are likewise
not independent replications: a single offset over a whole run would produce them. The change in
the power-law exponent, ∆n, is the slope of the paired log-ratio against ln v.


4 Data inventory and quality
Every set point of every rig run finished cleanly. The load ramp stopped on the power roll-off
each time, never on the current ceiling or a rotor stall, so every Pmax is a true maximum rather
than a lower bound. All rig runs carry protocol 94bed28333f7.
Within-mounting repeatability is good. Two single-point re-measurements of Ra 20 on the
same mounting (on a different protocol, so indicative only) matched the sweep to within 0.3% at
1200 and 1800 rpm.
Fan-speed logging changed between runs; the fan speed did not. The Ra 20 file records the
fan 4–21 rpm below its command, while Ra 40 and Ra 80 record it within 0–1 rpm. For Ra 20 the
software logged the drive’s output frequency multiplied by a fixed 29.5 rpm/Hz, and all 14 values
fall on that 0.1 Hz grid. From 25 August it read the drive’s own speed estimate. The fan motor
current at matched command agrees across the three runs to within 0.1 A, which a fan running up
to 21 rpm slower would not do. All comparisons are therefore made at matched commanded speed.
As a bound only: if the Ra 20 readback had been a real air-speed deficit, correcting for it would
reduce the gains to +4.3% and +9.4%.
Ambient conditions were recorded only for Ra 40 (24.6 °C, 101.04 kPa, sensor offset uncali-
brated). On this rig Pmax rises faster than linearly with dynamic pressure, so a density difference of
a few percent between days could shift Pmax by up to about twice that. This is small next to the
Ra 80 gain but comparable to the Ra 80-vs-Ra 40 step.

5.1 Power curves

Fig. 3 shows Pmax against wind speed. Peak power rises from about 0.03 W at 10.2 m/s to 3.79, 4.10
and 4.50 W at 38.0 m/s for Ra 20, Ra 40 and Ra 80. Each curve follows Pmax ∝ v n with n = 3.76,
3.77 and 3.79 (Table 5). Appendix A tabulates every set point.

5.2 Paired comparison

Table 4 and Fig. 1 give the paired comparisons. Individual set points scatter by about 4%: the
standard deviation of the log-ratios is 3.8% for Ra 40 and 4.7% for Ra 80. A single set point can
therefore show anything from +8.5% to +25.0% for Ra 80.
The largest deviations are at fan 1000–1100 (21 m/s to 23 m/s). At 1100 rpm both textured rotors
are high (+16.7% and +25.0% against Ra 20), which points to a low Ra 20 reading there. At 1000
rpm only Ra 80 is high: it is +24.6% over Ra 20 and +13.4% over Ra 40, so that point is specific to
Ra 80.

5.3 Trend with texture

Power increases with fuzzy-skin thickness at 13 of 14 set points (Fig. 4). The exception is 700 rpm,
where Ra 40 and Ra 80 are within 2.5%. A single slope in log–log coordinates gives Pmax ∝ tfuzz 0.092 ,

about +6.6% per doubling of thickness. No uncertainty can yet be attached to that slope. It rests
on three rotors mounted once each, and a 3% offset on the single Ra 20 mounting would move it by
about a quarter.

5.4 Where the extra power comes from

At a fixed wind speed the rotor, generator and rectifier together behave as a Thévenin source. Across
each load ladder the terminal voltage falls linearly with current, V = Voc − IRint , with r2 ≥ 0.975
at every set point of every run.
  • Voc is the zero-current intercept. It tracks the generator EMF, and hence rotor speed at light
    load, to within a rectifier offset of about 0.45 V.
  • Rint combines the winding, rectifier and wiring with the rotor’s slowing under load.
  • At the power maximum Pmax ≈ Voc       2 /4R , so a change in power can be split between the two.
                                               int

Fig. 5 and Table 8 show the result.
  • Open-circuit voltage. The textured rotors’ Voc is higher at every one of the 14 set points: by
    +4.1% ([+3.3, +4.9]) for Ra 40 and +6.4% ([+4.9, +7.9]) for Ra 80.
  • Source resistance. Their Rint is indistinguishable from Ra 20’s: −0.7% ([−2.7, +1.4]) and
    −0.4% ([−2.2, +1.5]). Within its uncertainty, the Voc change accounts for the whole power gain.
  • A direct check. The voltage measured at the first, lightest load step, with no fitting, is higher
    by +3.9% and +6.3%, at all 14 set points for both rotors.
At light load the textured rotors turn faster in the same wind, and the extra power
follows from that. The effect is on the rotor side of the generator, not in the electrical path.
The pattern differs between the two rotors. The Ra 40 advantage is flat across the range (+4.1%
below 17 m/s, +3.7% above 31 m/s). The Ra 80 advantage grows with wind speed, from +3.5% to
+7.8%: against Ra 40 its slope in ln v is +0.042 (p = 0.003). Faster free-running does not by itself
identify the mechanism. More aerodynamic torque and less parasitic torque would both produce it
(Section 7).

5.5 Scaling with wind speed

All rotors follow Pmax ∝ v n with n between 3.76 and 3.79 (Table 5). The paired exponent changes
are zero within their intervals: ∆n = +0.003 ± 0.058 for Ra 40 and +0.024 ± 0.071 for Ra 80.
That rules out one alternative explanation. Suppose the rotors differed in cut-in speed, for example
through constant friction. A cut-in model fitted to Ra 20, Pmax = a(v − vc )m with vc = 0.8 m/s and
m = 3.60, needs a shift of 0.72 m/s to reproduce the whole Ra 80 gain. That shift would change n
by −0.14, outside the measured interval. For Ra 40 the shift would be 0.45 m/s and the exponent
change −0.09, excluded only narrowly. A partial cut-in contribution of a few percent cannot be
excluded.
n ≈ 3.8 exceeds 3 because free-running speed rises faster than wind speed (Voc ∝ v 1.5 ) and the rotor
slows less under load at higher wind (Rint ∝ v −0.79 ). Both are properties of the rotor, including its
friction. That n ≈ 2(1.50) + 0.79 follows algebraically from the Thévenin form. It is a consistency
check, not evidence about which component sets the exponent.

6 Relation to the Jeong-lab tests
Two earlier datasets bear on the question: the initial reference of 5 June and the no-texture test of
27 July. Both predate the automated rig, whose first run was on 20 August, and both were recorded
on the Jeong-lab DAQ. The two setups use slightly different fan-to-wind calibrations (9.79 m/s
against 10.24 m/s at 500 rpm), so this section compares at matched fan set point. Fig. 7 shows both
datasets relative to the rig’s Ra 20.

6.1 Comparison with the initial test

Prof. Jeong asked on 27 July that new results be compared with the initial test. At 800–1700 rpm
the 5 June values lie between −6.6% and +25.3% of the rig’s Ra 20 (geometric mean +6.2%). At
500–700 rpm, where powers are below 0.15 W, they are +25.9 to +56.1% higher. Over the whole
range they sit about level with the rig’s Ra 80 (geometric mean +0.4%). The printed rotors therefore
perform about like the initial reference. The rotor, probably the original one, and the processing
differ, so this is a consistency check, not a texture comparison. Putting the June data on the same
basis as the rest would need the June raw export, which we would be glad to process together with
Taegu.

6.2 The 27 July no-texture test

We ran this test with the lab’s DAQ recording at 360 Hz. Because Taegu shared the complete raw
export, the lab’s Summary_Table.csv can be rebuilt from it exactly: all 64 values reproduce to
floating-point precision (Appendix C). The rebuild shows two things that matter for comparison
with the rig.
• The table’s columns are maxima of raw 360 Hz samples. The column names suggest
  the aim was the maximum power at each setting. Over raw samples, a maximum also collects
  channel noise, which here raises P by about 0.11 A × V . The rig reads one settled value per 1 s
  dwell.
• At 500–700 rpm the load was off. The tabulated power there reflects channel noise rather
  than delivered power. Those windows do give a clean free-running voltage: 3.11, 3.84 and 5.39 V.
  The rig’s Ra 20 reads 2.85, 4.47 and 5.62 V at its first load step. This comparison needs no
  current calibration, and it shows the two setups agree on rotor speed to within about 15%.
We then reprocessed the same windows on the rig’s basis: current zeroed at load-off, and a 1 s
mean before taking the maximum (the settled mean would be 2–11% lower still). On that basis
the no-texture values are below the rig’s Ra 20 at all 9 usable set points from 900 to 1800 rpm, by
9–18% at 1400–1800 rpm. This cannot be read as a texture effect:
• the two tests differ in print, mounting, day and electrical chain;
• the current scale (2 A/V, i.e. a 500 mV/A sensor) cannot be checked from the file, and a
  comparison of the lab’s and rig’s voltage–current lines suggests it may under-read, by enough to
  remove or reverse the gap.
What the July test can support is narrower: it cannot say whether the un-textured rotor is above
or below the textured ones at the 8–14% level.

7 Discussion
What is established. On one mounting of each rotor, peak electrical power increases with fuzzy-
skin thickness over 0.050 mm to 0.202 mm, and the increase is carried by the rotor’s free-running
speed rather than the electrical path.


                                                     11
Printed surface texture and VAWT electrical output                                     30 September 2026



The operating regime. The Jeong-lab pulse gives a loaded tip-speed ratio of λ ≈ 0.12–0.16.
Mapping the rig’s Voc through the generator constant from the lab’s unloaded windows gives a
free-running λ ≈ 0.11–0.22. Both are estimates, since the rig did not record rotor speed. Even
unloaded, then, the rotor turns far slower than a lift-driven rotor (λ ≈ 3–5): with its 183° bucket-like
blades it behaves as a drag-driven machine. This, more than generator loading, explains why the
electrical power coefficient at peak, Pmax /( 12 ρAv 3 ) with standard air density, is only 0.09–0.27%.
Mechanism. In a drag rotor the net torque is the difference between the drag on the concave
(advancing) and convex (returning) faces. On the concave face, separation is fixed by the sharp
edges. On the convex face it is not, and surface roughness can move it. That is the golf-ball effect,
and it would reduce the drag on the returning blade. The Ra 80 voltage advantage growing with
wind speed (Section 5.4) is what such a Reynolds-dependent effect would produce. It is a candidate,
not a demonstrated mechanism. Fuzzy skin also changes the printed shape slightly, which is a
second, geometric path. The Sandia study circulated in August [1] concerns airfoils at utility-scale
Reynolds numbers, where roughness is detrimental, so it is not the right reference for this rotor.
Alternative explanations, and how far the data exclude them.
• A one-off change between the first run and the others. Test order was Ra 20 (20 Aug), Ra 80 (26
  Aug), Ra 40 (1 Sep). That excludes a steady drift with date, but not a step. Ra 20 is the only
  run taken before a round of software and firmware changes and a drive-parameter restore (22–26
  Aug), so a step would lift both textured rotors above it. The comparison free of this confound is
  Ra 80 against Ra 40: +4.9% ([+1.5, +8.5]), with the Ra80 voltage advantage growing with wind.
  The remount of Ra 20 on current software tests the step directly.
• Friction and mounting. A constant-friction difference large enough to explain the gains would
  change the exponent (Section 5.5), and is excluded for Ra 80, narrowly for Ra 40. Losses that
  rise with rotor speed (viscous bearing drag, imbalance) change the exponent less, and the present
  intervals cannot exclude them. Remounting, and a spin-down test, would.
• Print differences other than texture. Mass, wall thickness, edge finish and the Ra 40 magnet
  (Section 2). Weighing the blades and reading the slicer files would bound these.
• Wind speed on the day. There is no independent wind measurement. A stronger wind would
  raise Voc and also lower Rint : by about −3.2% for the Ra 80 voltage change, against an observed
  −0.4% ([−2.2, +1.5]). That argues against a wind offset explaining all of the Ra 80 gain, but
  allows one explaining part of it. For Ra 40 the test does not discriminate.
• Fan-speed logging and air density. Discussed in Section 4.
Electrical power is not aerodynamic efficiency. The Thévenin analysis shows the textured
rotors spinning faster unloaded, but lower parasitic torque would produce exactly that, and the
rotors are compared at different tip-speed ratios. It is not a substitute for a Cp (λ) comparison,
which needs a trustworthy rotor-speed channel.

9 Recommended next steps
In order of value per tunnel-hour:
1. Remount each rotor, in randomised order. One remount of one rotor can detect a large
   mounting effect but cannot show that it is small, because it gives a single difference. If a remount
   moves Pmax by more than about 3%, the Ra 40-vs-Ra 20 and Ra 80-vs-Ra 40 differences are not
   established. Establishing the ordering needs a mount-to-mount standard deviation below about
   1.5–2%, measured from at least two remounts of each rotor.
2. Run the no-texture rotor on the rig, and Ra 10, to anchor the texture axis.
3. Record the lab DAQ in parallel during a rig sweep (Section 6). This calibrates the current
   channel and adds rotor speed.
4. Characterise the specimens: read the slicer files, weigh each blade set, and measure Ra.
5. Log ambient air and the anemometer on every run.

Observations from the rebuild.
• Current noise. The current channel carries about 31 mA of white noise, the same with the
  load on or off. At every window’s Pdc_max sample the current sits 0.095–0.144 A above its 1 s
  mean, so the noise adds roughly 0.11 A × V to each tabulated power. Applied to a stretch after
  the load was switched off, with the bus at 11–20 V, the same recipe returns 1.50 W.
• Load schedule. The load was held in constant current and raised by hand in a few steps per
  setting, and it was off for the first three settings.
• Window alignment. The processing assumes equal time at each setting. The settings were
  changed by hand (the drive automation came later), with dwells of about 12–38 s, so some
  windows straddle a change: the window labelled 1300 rpm contains the step to 1400 rpm.
• Pulse count. A few rising edges that span two samples are counted twice (five in all), and at
  2000 rpm the pulse misses about one revolution in eight: true rotor speed about 667 rpm against
  the 595 tabulated. At 1900 rpm and below the pulse tracks cleanly.
